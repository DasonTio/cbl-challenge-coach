## Purpose
Move from a vague problem instinct to one Challenge statement the learner actually owns (Nichols & Cator, 2008; Dikilitaş et al., 2025, Ch.2).

## Step 1: Surface a Big Idea
A Big Idea is a broad, evolving problem-space — not a specific proposal. In academic CBL these are typically assigned (climate change, health, sustainability); here the learner has to find their own. Any real-world friction, inefficiency, frustration, or shift can be a Big Idea, including ones that aren't obviously "a business" yet. Don't force business framing prematurely — help them articulate the problem-space first (e.g. "how food gets wasted between farms and stores," not "a logistics SaaS platform").

If they arrive with nothing: ask what keeps coming up for them — a frustration they've personally hit, an industry they know well, a change they've noticed. Don't hand them a generic list of startup categories to pick from; that produces an idea they don't own.

If they arrive with a Big Idea that's actually a specific solution ("I want to build X"), that's the most common Engage failure mode — see Anti-patterns below.

## Step 2: Generate essential questions
From the Big Idea, generate many candidate essential questions before narrowing. Mix question types — "what" and "when" surface facts; "why" and "what if" surface deeper framing. No question is unworthy at this stage. Write the full list into `challenge.md`, not just the winner — the discarded ones are useful context later.

## Step 3: Select one essential question
Narrow to the single essential question that (a) resonates most with the learner, not what sounds most impressive, and (b) is specific enough to act on. Ask directly: "if you could only answer one of these, which one would actually change what you do next?"

## Step 4: Formulate the Challenge
Convert the essential question into an actionable Challenge statement — typically a verb like *increase*, *reduce*, or *improve* plus a measurable target. Example: from "why does food get wasted between farms and stores?" to "reduce spoilage-driven food waste for small regional distributors."

## Exit criteria
The Challenge is:
- Specific enough to investigate (not so broad it could mean anything, not so narrow it forecloses exploration)
- Actionable and roughly measurable
- Owned by the learner — they can say why it matters to them, not just why it sounds good
- Not secretly a solution (see Anti-patterns)

It does **not** need to be evidence-tested yet — that's Investigate's job. Don't hold the Challenge to an Investigate-phase bar here.

## Anti-patterns and how to intervene
- **Vague** ("something about sustainability") — ask for a specific instance or story that made this real to them.
- **Oversized** ("fix climate change") — ask what slice of it they could actually move.
- **Predetermined / solution-disguised** ("build an app that helps people recycle") — the most common failure. The tell: the statement already contains a product, feature, or mechanism. Name it directly: "that's a solution to a problem — what's the problem underneath it?" Don't accept the challenge as stated, and don't silently rewrite it for them either — ask questions until *they* restate it as a problem.
